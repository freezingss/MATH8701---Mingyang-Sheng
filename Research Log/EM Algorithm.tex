\section{A Framework for Poisson Structure}
\subsection{Setup}
Let $\bm{y}_{ij} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^Q$ denote the count vector for observation $i$ in group $j(i)$ with $Q$ categories. For each observation $i$, we have an injection mapping $j(i) \in \{1, 2, \ldots, J\}$. Define $\mathcal{I}_j = \{i : j(i) = j\}$ and $I_j = |\mathcal{I}_j|$, so that the total number of observations satisfies $I = \sum_{j=1}^J I_j$. Conditional on the multinomial sums $y_{ij+}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}y_{ijq}$ and vector-valued random effect $\bm{\lambda}_{j}=(\lambda_{jq})_{q=1}^{Q}$ for group $j$, the counts $\bm{y}_{ij}$ are independently multinomial distributed for each observation $i$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \bm{y}_{ij} \mid  y_{ij+} , \bm{\lambda}_{ij}
    \sim \operatorname{Multinomial}\left(y_{ij+},\, \bm{p}_{ij}\right),
\end{equation}
Given the random effects, we treat $y_{ij+}$ as random and assume
\begin{equation}
    y_{ij+}\mid \bm{\lambda}_{j}\sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\delta_{ij}\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\lambda_{jq}\zeta_{ijq}),
\end{equation}
independently for each $i$ and $j$. The result given by \cite{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting} shows
\begin{equation}
    y_{ijq}\mid \lambda_{jq}\sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\delta_{ij}\lambda_{jq}\zeta_{iq}),
\end{equation}
independently for each $i$ and $q$. Random effects are unobserved naturally, thus \citet{lee2017poissontrickextensionsfitting} obtained the marginal distribution by integrating out the random effects and expressed the closed-form of the marginal likelihood of the approximating Poisson surrogate model with the assumption for the gamma distribution of the random effects. Our approach takes a fundamentally different route rather than marginalizing out the random effects. We extend the pPCA method on the distribution of poissons and assume \cite{chiquet2018variational}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item there exists a (low) $k$-dimensional (linear) subspace in the natural canonical parameter space where some latent variable $\bm{z}_{ij}$ lies,
    \item observations $y_{ijq}$ are independently generated in observation space with $y_{ijq}\mid z_{ijq}\sim \operatorname{Poisson}\bigl(\exp(z_{ijq})\bigr)$.
\end{itemize}
The latter is linked to $\mathbb{E}[\bm{y}_{ij}\mid \bm{z}_{ij}]$ through the canonical link function $g$, which uses the similar connection in generalized linear models. Specifically, the latent variable $z_{ijq}$ corresponds to $\log \lambda_{jq}$ since the canonical link function for the poisson family is the logarithm. Therefore, we place a factor structure on the log random effects at the group level based on the assumption in \cite{chiquet2018variational}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        & \log \bm{\lambda}_{j}=\bm{\mu}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{f}_j+\bm{\epsilon}_{j},\\
        & \mathbf{f}_j \sim \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{0}, \bm{I}_k),\\
        & \bm{\epsilon}_j \sim \mathcal{N}_Q(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{D}),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation} 
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots ,\mu_{Q})^{\top}\in \mathbb{R}^{Q}$ denotes the population-level baseline log-intensities for each category,
\item $\mathbf{B}=[\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\dots ,\boldsymbol{b}_{K}]\in \mathbb{R}^{Q\times K}$ denotes the factor loading matrix with $K\ll Q$ and its entries $\mathbf{B}_{qk}$ denote the loading of category $q$'s random effect to the $k$-th latent factor,
\item $\bm{f}_{j}\in \mathbb{R}^{K}$ denotes the group-specific latent factor scores distributed identically and independently,
\item $\bm{\epsilon}_{j}\in \mathbb{R}^{Q}$ denotes the error term that captures category-specific variation in $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}$ that is not attributable to $K$ latent factors. 
\end{itemize}
This structure captures the systematic co-variation of probabilities across categories within a group. A natural starting point is to model $\bm{\lambda}_{j}=(\lambda_{j1},\dots ,\lambda_{jQ})^{\top}$ with $Q$ independent random effects. However, this assumption becomes increasingly restrictive as $Q$ grows. Independence implies a diagonal cross-category covariance structure for $\bm{\lambda}_{j}$, which rules out the systematic co-variance of the probabilities that occurs in high-dimensional data. We relax this assumption by positing that the cross-category co-variation is driven by a smaller number of latent factors rather than being independently determined for each categories. 
Marginalizing over $\bm{f}_{j}$, the marginal distribution of $\log \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}$ is:
\begin{equation}
		\log \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}\sim \mathcal{N}_{Q}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}),\quad \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}+\mathbf{D}
\end{equation}
Also, the parameter $\delta_{ij}$ is a known offset and $\zeta_{iq}$ is a positive individual-specified function of covariate $\bm{v}_{i}$ and fixed effect $\bm{\gamma}$.

\subsection{Algorithm}
Let $\mathbf{B}=[\bm{b}_{1},\cdots, \bm{b}_{K}]$ and parameter space $\Theta=\{\bm{\mu},\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{D}\}$. Before introducing our approach, we recall the classic EM algorithm. In latent variable models, it is common to assume we can only obtain samples from $\bm{y}$ while $\bm{f}$, called latent variable, cannot be observed. Consider the marginal distribution of $\bm{y}$ as
\begin{equation}
    y_{\Theta}(\bm{y}):= \int_{\mathcal{F}} p_{\Theta}(\bm{y},\bm{f})\, d\bm{f}.
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    p_{\Theta}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{f}_{j})=p(\bm{f}_{j})\cdot \prod_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{j}} p(\bm{y}_{ij}\mid \bm{f}_{j})
\end{equation}
We want to estimate the model parameter $\Theta$, so we consider the maximum likelihood estimation on $J$ groups level
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Theta}=\arg\max\limits_{\Theta \in \Omega}h(\Theta; \bm{y}_{1},\cdots,\bm{y}_{J}),
    \label{eq:mle1}
\end{equation}
where we take the average value of groups to reduce the quantity of log-likelihood sum
\begin{equation}
    h(\Theta; \bm{y}_{1},\cdots,\bm{y}_{J}):=\dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \log y_{\Theta}(\bm{y}_{j}).
\end{equation}
In many settings, the objective function \ref{eq:mle1} is highly nonconvex and thus computationally inefficient to solve directly. Instead, we consider the lower bound of $ h(\Theta; \bm{y}_{1},\cdots,\bm{y}_{J})$ which is more suitable to evaluate and optimize. Let $f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{ij})$ denote the conditional distribution of the latent variable $\bm{f}_{j}$ given the observation $\bm{y}_{ij}$ and the complete observation at the group-level $\bm{y}_{j}:=\{\bm{y}_{ij}\}_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{j}}$. For any $\tilde{\Theta}\in \Omega$, we have 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    h(\tilde{\Theta}; \bm{y}_{1},\cdots,\bm{y}_{J})&=\dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \log y_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\bm{y}_{j})=\dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \log \int_{\mathcal{F}} \Bigl[\prod_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{j}}p_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\bm{y}_{ij}\mid \bm{f}_{j})\Bigr]p(\bm{f}_{j})\, d\bm{f}_{j}\\
    &= \dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \log \int_{\mathcal{F}} f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})\dfrac{p_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{f}_{j})}{f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})}\,d\bm{f}_{j}\\
    &\geq \dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \int_{\mathcal{F}} f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})\log \dfrac{p_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{f}_{j})}{f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})}\,d\bm{f}_{j}\\
    &= \dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \Bigl[\int_{\mathcal{F}} f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})\log p_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{f}_{j})\,d\bm{f}_{j}-\int_{\mathcal{F}} f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})\log f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j},\bm{y}_{j})\,d\bm{f}_{j}\Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the inequality follows from Jensen's inequality. \textcolor{red}{This proof needs to clarify conditional independence assumption that $\bm{y}_{ij}\perp\!\!\!\perp \bm{y}_{i'j} \mid \bm{f}_{j}$ for any $i\neq i'\in \mathcal{I}_{j}$.} Notice that only the first term contains parameter $\tilde{\Theta}$, we define it as the Q-function, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    Q_{J}(\tilde{\Theta}\mid \Theta):=\dfrac{1}{J} \sum_{j=1}^{J} \int_{\mathcal{F}} f_{\Theta}(\bm{f}_{j}\mid \bm{y}_{j})\log p_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\bm{y}_{j},\bm{f}_{j})\,d\bm{f}_{j}.
    \label{eq: Q_function}
\end{equation}
One iteration of EM algorithm mapping $\Theta^{(t)}$ to $\Theta^{(t+1)}$ consists the following two steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item E-step: Compute the function $ Q_{J}(\Theta\mid \Theta^{(t)})$ given $\Theta^{(t)}$.
    \item M-step: Compute $\Theta^{(t+1)}=\arg\max\limits_{\Theta \in \Omega}Q_{J}(\Theta\mid \Theta^{(t)})$.
\end{itemize}
The mapping $\mathcal{M}_{J}:\Omega \to \Omega$ denotes the above algorithm
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_{J}(\Theta):=\arg\max\limits_{\tilde{\Theta} \in \Omega}Q_{J}(\tilde{\Theta}\mid \Theta).
\end{equation}
and the mapping of the population level $\mathcal{M}:\Omega \to \Omega$ when $J\to \infty$ as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}(\Theta):=\arg\max\limits_{\tilde{\Theta} \in \Omega}Q_{J}(\tilde{\Theta}\mid \Theta).
\end{equation}
Generally, \cite{yi2015regularizedemalgorithmsunified} established two reasons that the classic EM procedure is not applicable to the high-dimensional case where $QK\gg J$. First, the group-level M-step $\mathcal{M}_{J}(\Theta)$ is usually far from the population-level mapping $\mathcal{M}(\Theta)$ with an insufficient number of groups relative to the number of categories. Even if the initial parameter $\Theta^{*}$, $\mathcal{M}_{J}(\Theta^{*})$ is not a meaningful estimation of $\Theta^{*}$ as the estimation error $\lVert \mathcal{M}_{J}(\Theta^{*})-\mathcal{M}(\Theta^{*}) \rVert_F$ can be much larger than the signal strength $\lVert \Theta^{*} \rVert_F$.  Second, in some models, $\mathcal{M}_{J}(\Theta)$ is not even well-defined.
Thus, we introduce the regularized EM algorithm that is designed to overcome challenges of high dimension.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{High Dimensional Regularized EM Algorithm \cite{yi2015regularizedemalgorithmsunified}} 
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
 
\Require Observations $\{\bm{y}_{ij}\}_{i\in \mathcal{I}_{j},j=1,\cdots,J}$, regularizer $R$, number of iterations $T$, 
initial parameter $\Theta^{(0)}=\{\bm{\mu}^{(0)},\mathbf{B}^{(0)},\mathbf{D}^{(0)}\}$, initial regularization parameter $\vartheta_{J}^{(0)}$, 
estimated statistical error $\Delta$, contractive factor $\kappa < 1$.

\For{$t = 1,2,\ldots,T$}

\State \textbf{Regularization parameter update:}
\[
\vartheta_{J}^{(t)} \leftarrow \kappa \vartheta_{J}^{(t-1)} + \Delta .
\]

\State \textbf{E-step:} Compute $Q_{J}(\Theta \mid \Theta^{(t-1)})$ given $\Theta^{(t-1)}$ according to \ref{eq: Q_function}.

\State \textbf{Regularized M-step:}
\[
\Theta^{(t)}
\leftarrow
\arg\max_{\Theta \in \Omega}
Q_{J}(\Theta \mid \Theta^{(t-1)})
-
\vartheta_{J}^{(t)} \cdot R(\Theta).
\]

\EndFor

\Ensure $\Theta^{(T)}$.

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The proposed algorithm improves upon the high-dimensional EM algorithm of \cite{wang2015highdimensionalexpectationmaximizationalgorithm}, which implements the M-step via gradient ascent when the $\mathcal{Q}$ function does not admit a closed form. In contrast, our formulation exploits the block decomposition to obtain exact closed form updates for $\mathbf{D}$ and a structured proximal Newton step for $(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{B})$.

Given the regularization parameter $\vartheta_{J}$, our regularized M-step is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_{J}^{r}(\Theta):=\arg\max_{\Theta \in \Omega}
Q_{J}(\tilde{\Theta} \mid \Theta)-\vartheta_{J} \cdot \mathcal{R}(\tilde{\Theta}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}$ encourages a certain structure in the model parameter $\Theta^{*}$.

The choice of regularization parameter $\vartheta_{J}^{(t)}$ plays an important role in controlling statistical and optimization error. \cite{yi2015regularizedemalgorithmsunified} used the quantity $\Delta=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\log p/n}\bigr)$ as default to show the target estimate error, where $p$ denotes the dimension of the data and $n$ denotes the number of observations. Following \cite{yi2015regularizedemalgorithmsunified}, we set $\Delta_{\bm{\mu},\mathbf{B}}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\sqrt{\log Q/J}\bigr)$ as the target estimation error for $(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{B})$. This rate reflects a union bound over the Q component parameters and is consistent with the high-dimensional GLM error bounds of \cite{Negahban_2012}. Hence, the update of the parameter of penalty is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \vartheta_{J}^{(t)}=\kappa^{t}\vartheta_{J}^{(0)}+\dfrac{1-\kappa^{t}}{1-\kappa}\Delta_{\bm{\mu},\mathbf{B}}.
\end{equation}
where $\kappa\in (0,1)$ is the contractive factor governing the geometric decay of the optimization error across iterations.

\begin{proposition}[Block decomposition of the M-step]
\label{prop:block_decomp}
Given the E-step output $\{\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j\}_{j=1}^J$, the Q-function admits the decomposition
\begin{equation}
    Q_J(\tilde{\Theta} \mid \Theta^{(t)})
    = Q_J^{\mu,B}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{B} \mid \mathbf{D}^{(t)})
    + Q_J^{D}(\mathbf{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)},
      \mathbf{B}^{(t+1)})
    + \mathrm{const},
\end{equation}
where the two components contain only the parameters $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{B})$ and $\mathbf{D}$ respectively, with no shared free parameters between them.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}

\textbf{Step 1: Expansion of the Q-function.}

By definition,
\begin{equation}
    Q_J(\Theta \mid \Theta^{(t)})
    = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^J \int_{\mathcal{F}}
      f_{\Theta^{(t)}}(\mathbf{f}_j \mid \mathbf{y}_j)\,
      \log p_{\Theta}(\mathbf{y}_j, \mathbf{f}_j)
      \, d\mathbf{f}_j.
\end{equation}
Decomposing the joint log-likelihood as
\begin{equation}
    \log p_{\Theta}(\mathbf{y}_j, \mathbf{f}_j)
    = \log p_{\Theta}(\mathbf{y}_j \mid \mathbf{f}_j)
    + \log p(\mathbf{f}_j),
\end{equation}
the factor prior contributes $\log p(\mathbf{f}_j) = -\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{f}_j\|^2 + \mathrm{const}$, which is free of $\tilde{\Theta}$ and is absorbed into the constant term. For conditional likelihood, integrating out $\lambda_{jq}$ given $\mathbf{f}_j$ via the log-normal moment-generating function $\mathbb{E}[e^{\epsilon_{jq}}] = e^{d_q^2/2}$ for $\epsilon_{jq} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, d_q^2)$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \log p_{\tilde{\Theta}}(\mathbf{y}_j \mid \mathbf{f}_j)
    = \sum_{i=1}^{n_j}\sum_{q=1}^Q
      \Bigl[y_{ijq}\bigl(\mu_q + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\mathbf{f}_j\bigr)
      - \delta_{ij}\zeta_{ijq}\,
      e^{\mu_q + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\mathbf{f}_j + d_q^2/2}
      \Bigr].
\end{equation}
Substituting into the Q-function and replacing $\mathbf{f}_j$ by the Laplace approximation sufficient statistic $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j \approx
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{f}_j \mid \mathbf{y}_j, \Theta^{(t)}]$ yields
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Q_expanded}
    Q_J(\Theta \mid \Theta^{(t)})
    = \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j,i,q}
      \Bigl[y_{ijq}\bigl(\mu_q + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j\bigr)
      - \delta_{ij}\zeta_{ijq}\,
      e^{\mu_q + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j + d_q^2/2}
      \Bigr]
    + \mathrm{const}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2: Identifying the separation structure.}

In \eqref{eq:Q_expanded}, the term $d_q^2$ appears exclusively as the multiplicative scalar $e^{d_q^2/2}$ within the exponential, coupled with $(\mu_q, \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot})$. Under sequential coordinate ascent, fixing $\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{D}^{(t)}$ renders each $e^{d_q^{2(t)}/2}$ a known constant. The component of \eqref{eq:Q_expanded} involving only $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{B})$ is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Q_muB}
    Q_J^{\mu,B}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{B} \mid \mathbf{D}^{(t)})
    := \frac{1}{J}\sum_{j,i,q}
       \Bigl[y_{ijq}\bigl(\mu_q + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j\bigr)
       - \delta_{ij}\zeta_{ijq}\,
       e^{\mu_q + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j
       + d_q^{2(t)}/2}\Bigr],
\end{equation}
which contains no free parameters from $\mathbf{D}$.

After substituting $(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)}, \mathbf{B}^{(t+1)})$, the contribution of $\mathbf{D}$ arises from the complete-data log-likelihood term associated with $\epsilon_{jq} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, d_q^2)$, giving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Q_D}
    Q_J^{D}(\mathbf{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)},
    \mathbf{B}^{(t+1)})
    := -\frac{1}{2J}\sum_{q=1}^Q
       \left[\frac{\mathcal{S}_q^{(t+1)}}{d_q^2}
       + J\log d_q^2\right],
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}_q^{(t+1)}
    = \sum_{j=1}^J\left[
      \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}^{(t+1)}
      \hat{\mathbf{H}}_j^{-1}
      \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}^{(t+1)\top}
      + \Bigl(\mu_q^{(t+1)}
      + \mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}^{(t+1)}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}_j
      - s_{jq}\Bigr)^2\right],
\end{equation}
with $s_{jq} = \log\hat{\lambda}_{jq}^{(t)}$, and \eqref{eq:Q_D} contains no free parameters from $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{B})$.

\textbf{Step 3: The decomposition holds.}

From \eqref{eq:Q_muB} and \eqref{eq:Q_D},
\begin{equation}
    Q_J(\tilde{\Theta} \mid \Theta^{(t)})
    = Q_J^{\mu,B}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{B} \mid \mathbf{D}^{(t)})
    + Q_J^{D}(\mathbf{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(t+1)},
      \mathbf{B}^{(t+1)})
    + \mathrm{const},
\end{equation}
with no shared free parameters between the two components. Furthermore, the penalty term $\vartheta_{J}^{(t)}\sum_{q=1}^Q\|\mathbf{B}_{q\cdot}\|_2$ contains only parameters from $\mathbf{B}$ and is therefore absorbed into $Q_J^{\mu,B}$, leaving the separation structure intact.
\end{proof}

\section{Identifiability Theory}
\subsection{Overview and Structure of the Identifiability Problem}
The loading matrix $\mathbf{B}$ is convenient for optimization and visualization but is not identifiable per se. Any orthogonal transformation $\mathbf{B} \mapsto \mathbf{BR}$ for orthodolody matrix $\mathbf{R}$ leads to the same model, since $\mathbf{BR}(\mathbf{BR})^\top = \mathbf{BB}^\top$. The identifiable quantity is therefore $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{BB}^\top + \mathbf{D}$, which decomposes into a rank-$K$ component capturing cross-category co-variation driven by latent factors and a diagonal component $\mathbf{D} = \mathrm{diag}(d_1^2, \ldots, d_Q^2)$ capturing category-specific idiosyncratic variation not attributable to the latent factors. This decomposition distinguishes our model from \citet{chiquet2018variational}, who specify $\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{BB}^\top$ without the diagonal term. 

Determine the matrices given $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a classic problem. \cite{Anderson1956StatisticalII} imposed a necessary condition for identification for $\mathbf{D}$ is that each column of loading matrix $\mathbf{B}$ has at least three nonzero elements. That requires the category number $p$ is greater than the latent factor number $k$ plus 2, which is satisfies automatically under the high-dimensional setup. Also, we assume that the diagonal entries of $\mathbf{D}$ are strictly positive, which avoids the Heywood case. The identification problem of $\mathbf{B}$ can be determined uniquely based on our assumption that the upper triangular entries of $\mathbf{B}$ are set to zero and the diagonal entries are positive.

The inclusion of $\mathbf{D}$ offers two advantages. First, it yields a richer covariance structure: the pure low-rank specification forces all residual category-specific variance to be absorbed into the factor loadings, whereas $\mathbf{D}$ explicitly accounts for the heterogeneity in baseline variability across categories. Second, since $\mathbf{BB}^\top + \mathbf{D}$ is full-rank whenever $d_q^2 > 0$ for all $q$, the identifiability condition $Q \geq 2K + 1$ is more robustly satisfied than in the pure low-rank case. This structure corresponds exactly to the covariance decomposition of classical factor analysis \cite{Anderson1956StatisticalII}, in contrast to the PCA-type decomposition of \citet{chiquet2018variational}.

Unlike standard PCA, there is no analogue of the \textbf{Eckart--Young} theorem for the Poisson exponential family, so orthogonality constraints on $\mathbf{B}$ would not yield computational simplifications. The lower-triangular constraint is therefore imposed purely for identifiability, not for computational convenience.
